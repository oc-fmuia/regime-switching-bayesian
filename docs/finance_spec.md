## 1. The Case Against Single-Regime Models

The standard approach to portfolio construction and risk management begins with a simple premise: estimate a mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and a covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ from historical returns, then feed these into an optimiser. The procedure implicitly assumes that the data were generated by a single statistical process, i.e. that the distribution of returns in January 2008 and January 2017 was the same, and that a long enough estimation window will average out any local fluctuations. This assumption is demonstrably wrong, and the consequences for investors are asymmetric: the model fails most badly precisely when correct risk measurement matters most.

Consider the experience of a portfolio manager running a mean-variance optimiser on monthly equity returns from 2003 to 2008. The five-year rolling window captures the mid-2000s expansion, during which the S&P 500 delivered roughly 10--12% annualised returns with volatility near 10%. The optimiser produces large equity allocations because the estimated Sharpe ratio is attractive and the covariance matrix suggests moderate risk. Then the financial crisis arrives. Realised volatility triples. Correlations between equity markets, previously moderate, converge toward one. Drawdowns exceed 50%. The single-regime model, calibrated to a world that no longer exists, has systematically understated the probability and severity of exactly the scenario that materialises.

The problem is not that the model was poorly estimated; it is that the data-generating process changed. Financial returns do not come from one distribution. They come from at least two: a growth environment characterised by positive drift, compressed volatility, and moderate cross-asset correlations, and a stress environment characterised by negative drift, elevated volatility, and correlation convergence. Averaging across these environments produces parameter estimates that are wrong in both: overstating volatility during calm markets (leaving returns on the table) and understating it during crises (underpricing tail risk).

This observation has a long empirical pedigree. Hamilton (1989) introduced the Markov-switching framework for business-cycle analysis, showing that US GDP growth was well described by a two-state model in which the economy alternated between expansion and recession, with distinct growth rates and volatilities in each state. Ang and Bekaert (2002) extended this framework to international equity markets and demonstrated that accounting for regimes materially changes both optimal asset allocation and the measurement of risk. Their 2004 paper in the *Financial Analysts Journal* showed that a regime-switching model with just two states and no covariates produced meaningfully different portfolio weights from a single-regime model, and that the differences were largest during the periods when they mattered most. The evidence for regime-dependent behaviour in equity markets is not a statistical curiosity; it is a first-order feature of the data that any serious risk framework must accommodate.

## 2. What Regimes Are and Why They Exist

A regime, in the context of this model, is a persistent market state characterised by a distinct statistical signature: its own expected return vector, its own volatility level, and, when the model is extended, its own correlation structure. The two-regime case corresponds to a bull market and a bear market, but these are not arbitrary labels. They reflect genuinely different economic environments, driven by distinct mechanisms, and the statistical differences between them are large enough to dominate portfolio risk.

### The statistical fingerprint of regimes

The bull regime is characterised by moderate positive drift (typically 8--15% annualised for broad equity indices), low volatility (10--15% annualised), and cross-asset correlations that reflect normal diversification benefits. Returns in this regime cluster around their mean, and large daily or monthly moves are relatively rare. The bear regime, by contrast, features negative drift (returns that are on average losing money), sharply elevated volatility (25--40% annualised, roughly two to three times the bull level), and a collapse in diversification as correlations between equity markets rise toward one. This asymmetry is not symmetric in time either: bull regimes tend to be long-lived (lasting years), while bear regimes are shorter (lasting months) but devastating in their impact on portfolio value.

These stylised facts are remarkably stable across markets and time periods. The 1987 crash, the 1997 Asian crisis, the 2000--2002 dot-com bust, the 2008--2009 global financial crisis, and the 2020 COVID crash all share the same fingerprint: a sudden increase in volatility, a shift to negative expected returns, and a breakdown of cross-asset diversification. The bull periods between them (the late 1990s expansion, the 2003--2007 recovery, the 2009--2020 bull market) all share the opposite characteristics. The persistence of these patterns across decades and geographies is what makes regime-switching models empirically useful rather than merely theoretically elegant.

### Why markets exhibit persistent states

The persistence of regimes, i.e. the fact that markets tend to remain in bull or bear states for extended periods rather than switching randomly from month to month, has deep roots in the economics of financial markets. Several reinforcing mechanisms contribute.

First, business cycles themselves are persistent. Expansions build on positive feedback loops: rising employment generates consumer spending, which drives corporate revenue, which supports hiring and investment. Recessions work through the same channels in reverse, with additional amplification from financial stress (credit tightening, forced selling, liquidity withdrawal). These real-economy dynamics create sustained periods of either positive or negative fundamental news for equity markets.

Second, financial markets exhibit self-reinforcing dynamics that amplify fundamental shocks. Rising prices increase the value of collateral, relaxing borrowing constraints and enabling further risk-taking. Falling prices trigger margin calls, forced selling, and liquidity spirals. Volatility itself is autocorrelated (the phenomenon known as volatility clustering) because a large shock, regardless of its sign, increases uncertainty and the probability of further large moves. The GARCH literature has documented this effect extensively, but GARCH models capture it through a single-regime autoregressive process. A regime-switching model captures it more directly: the bear regime *is* the high-volatility state, and the transition matrix governs how long the market tends to remain there.

Third, investor behaviour contributes to regime persistence. Behavioural finance has documented herding, panic selling, and slow adjustment to new information. In a crisis, risk aversion rises across the investor base simultaneously, creating a feedback loop between falling prices and increasing desire to sell. In expansions, gradual allocation back to equities sustains demand. These behavioural dynamics reinforce the persistence already present in fundamentals and market microstructure.

The transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ captures this persistence parsimoniously. When the diagonal elements $P_{kk}$ are large (e.g. 0.95), the expected duration of regime $k$ is $1/(1 - P_{kk}) = 20$ months. The off-diagonal elements govern the switching rate. A well-estimated transition matrix encodes, in two numbers per regime, the market's empirical tendency to remain in each state and its propensity to switch. This information is directly useful for risk management and allocation.

### Why volatility clusters

Volatility clustering, i.e. the tendency for large returns (of either sign) to be followed by large returns, and small returns by small returns, is one of the most robust empirical features of financial time series. Mandelbrot (1963) first documented it, and it has been confirmed across every asset class, market, and time period subsequently studied. Within a single-regime framework, volatility clustering is modelled through autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH). But a regime-switching model offers a complementary explanation: volatility clusters because markets spend extended periods in the high-volatility bear regime before transitioning back to the low-volatility bull regime. The aggregate time series, which mixes draws from both regimes, exhibits the appearance of time-varying volatility even though each regime has a fixed volatility level. In reality, both mechanisms are at work: there is genuine intra-regime volatility dynamics *and* cross-regime volatility differences, which is why the extensions described in Section 7 incorporate autoregressive dynamics within each regime.

## 3. Regime Uncertainty and Portfolio Risk

The deepest consequence of ignoring regimes is not that risk estimates are noisy; it is that they are systematically biased, and the bias is worst precisely when accurate risk measurement is most important. Understanding this asymmetry is essential for any practitioner considering a regime-switching framework.

### How single-regime models understate crisis risk

A single-regime model estimates one covariance matrix from the full sample. If the sample contains 80% bull months and 20% bear months (a typical ratio given that expansions last longer than recessions), the estimated covariance matrix is a weighted average that is dominated by the calm-market data. Monthly portfolio volatility might be estimated at 4%, corresponding to an annualised figure of roughly 14%. This estimate is roughly correct on average, but it understates risk during bear markets by a factor of two or more. A monthly VaR calculated from this covariance matrix will be exceeded far more often during stress periods than the nominal confidence level suggests, precisely because the VaR was calibrated to a mixture that dilutes the stress-period signal.

The problem is compounded by correlations. During bull markets, a portfolio of US, EAFE (developed international), and EM (emerging market) equities benefits from moderate diversification, with correlations typically in the 0.5--0.7 range, and the portfolio's volatility is meaningfully lower than the weighted average of its components' volatilities. During bear markets, correlations converge toward one. The diversification benefit evaporates exactly when it is needed most. A single-regime model that estimates correlations from the full sample will find moderate values (perhaps 0.6) and conclude that diversification works. It does, but only in the regime where it is least needed.

### The Bayesian perspective on regime uncertainty

A Bayesian regime-switching model addresses these problems by producing, at every point in time, a probability distribution over regimes rather than a binary label. If the model assigns a 70% probability to the bull regime and a 30% probability to the bear regime, the portfolio's risk is computed as a mixture:

$$
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{eff}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k + \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \, (\boldsymbol{\mu}_k - \bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k - \bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^\top
$$

where $p_k$ is the probability of regime $k$, $\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \sum_k p_k \boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ is the mixture mean, and the second term captures the additional variance arising from uncertainty about which regime's mean applies. This effective covariance matrix is larger than either regime's individual covariance matrix whenever the regime means differ, which they always do in practice. Even a small probability of being in the bear regime (say 15%) inflates the effective covariance meaningfully, because the bear-regime volatilities and the cross-regime mean differences are both large.

This is the core value proposition of a regime-switching risk model: it automatically increases risk estimates as the probability of the stress regime rises, without waiting for the crisis to fully materialise. A single-regime model can only increase its risk estimate after enough high-volatility observations have entered the estimation window, by which time the drawdown has already occurred. The regime-switching model, by contrast, begins increasing risk estimates as soon as the forward filter assigns non-trivial probability to the bear state, often at the first signs of stress, well before the full drawdown has materialised.

### Parameter uncertainty as risk

The Bayesian framework adds a second layer of conservatism: uncertainty about the model parameters themselves. The posterior distribution over regime means, volatilities, and transition probabilities reflects the finite-sample uncertainty in all estimated quantities. A portfolio decision that integrates over this posterior, rather than plugging in point estimates, is automatically more cautious, because it accounts for the possibility that the true parameters are less favourable than the posterior mean suggests. This is particularly important for the bear-regime parameters, which are estimated from fewer observations (bear markets are shorter) and therefore carry wider credible intervals. A Bayesian portfolio optimiser that integrates over these uncertain bear-regime parameters will allocate more conservatively than one that uses point estimates, because the tails of the parameter posterior include scenarios where the bear regime is even worse than the posterior mean suggests.

## 4. Smoothed vs Filtered Probabilities: The Practitioner's Distinction

The model produces two kinds of regime probabilities, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in applying regime-switching models to portfolio decisions. The distinction is not merely technical; it determines whether the resulting strategy is investable or suffers from look-ahead bias.

### Smoothed probabilities: the retrospective view

Smoothed probabilities $P(s_t = k \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T})$ condition on the *entire* sample, including data from after time $t$. They are computed by the forward-filter backward-sampler (FFBS), which first runs a forward pass to compute filtered probabilities and then samples backward through the regime sequence, allowing future observations to refine the estimate of past regimes. Because they use future information, smoothed probabilities are typically sharper: they assign probabilities closer to zero or one, and they detect regime transitions more quickly, because the backward pass can see what happened next.

Smoothed probabilities are the correct tool for model validation. When working with synthetic data where the true regime sequence is known, comparing the smoothed regime assignments against ground truth verifies that the model is correctly recovering the latent structure. When working with historical data, smoothed probabilities provide the best retrospective narrative of market environments, useful for understanding what happened and why, for calibrating intuition about regime characteristics, and for communicating with stakeholders who want to understand the model's view of history.

Smoothed probabilities must never be used for allocation decisions or backtesting. A strategy that conditions on future data cannot be executed in real time, and backtesting such a strategy produces artificially good results because the "predictions" are contaminated by future information. This is not a subtle bias; it can be the difference between a strategy that appears to halve drawdowns and one that adds no value relative to a static benchmark.

### Filtered probabilities: the causal, investable signal

Filtered probabilities $P(s_t = k \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ condition only on data available up to and including time $t$. At each month, the forward filter processes the new observation and updates the regime probability using Bayes' rule:

$$
P(s_t = k \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}) \propto p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid s_t = k) \sum_{j} P_{jk} \, P(s_{t-1} = j \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})
$$

This recursion is causal: the filtered probability at time $t$ depends only on the history up to $t$ and the model parameters. If the parameters themselves are estimated from data up to time $t$ (as in a walk-forward procedure), the entire signal is causal and the resulting strategy is investable.

Filtered probabilities are noisier than smoothed probabilities. They respond to regime transitions with a delay, because the model needs a few observations from the new regime before the likelihood tips the balance. A transition from bull to bear might take two to four months to be detected with high confidence via filtering, whereas the smoother, looking backward from later months of clearly elevated volatility, will identify the transition more precisely. This delay is not a flaw; it is the honest cost of making decisions without future information. A filtered probability of 0.5 at a regime boundary is the model saying, truthfully, that the data accumulated so far are ambiguous. Any allocation strategy must be comfortable operating under this kind of uncertainty.

### The allocation timing question

For allocation purposes, the strategy implemented in this framework uses the filtered probability from the *previous* month to determine the equity weight for the current month:

$$
w_t^{\text{equity}} = f\bigl(P(s_{t-1} = \text{Bear} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})\bigr)
$$

This one-month lag ensures that the weight is fully determined before observing the return it applies to. Even within the filtered framework, using $P(s_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ to determine the weight applied to $r_t$ would be subtly forward-looking, because $\mathbf{y}_t$ contains $r_t$. The one-month lag eliminates this concern entirely.

## 5. Regime-Aware Portfolio Allocation

### The risk-management philosophy

The regime-aware allocation strategy implemented in this framework is designed as a risk-management tool, not an alpha generator. This distinction matters. An alpha-generation strategy aims to increase expected returns by predicting market direction; a risk-management strategy aims to improve the risk-return trade-off by reducing exposure during periods of elevated risk. The two goals are related but distinct, and their evaluation criteria differ.

The core insight is that bear markets combine two features that are individually damaging and jointly devastating for a fully invested equity portfolio: negative expected returns and high volatility. A static allocation that is calibrated to deliver an acceptable risk level on average will be under-risked during bull markets (missing potential returns) and over-risked during bear markets (suffering unnecessary drawdowns). A regime-aware strategy that shifts between two weight profiles (aggressive during bull markets and defensive during bear markets) can improve the risk-adjusted return without necessarily improving the total return.

### The allocation rule

The current implementation uses a binary rule with two weight profiles applied to an equal-weight portfolio of equity indices:

- **Bull signal** ($P(s_{t-1} = \text{Bear} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) \leq 0.5$): allocate 80% to equities and 20% to a risk-free asset earning 4% per annum (approximately 0.33% per month).

- **Bear signal** ($P(s_{t-1} = \text{Bear} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) > 0.5$): allocate 20% to equities and 80% to the risk-free asset.

The portfolio return at time $t$ is:

$$
r_t^{\text{port}} = w_t^{\text{equity}} \cdot \bar{r}_t^{\text{eq}} + (1 - w_t^{\text{equity}}) \cdot r^{\text{rf}}_{\text{monthly}}
$$

where $\bar{r}_t^{\text{eq}}$ is the equal-weight average return across the equity indices.

### Why asymmetric weights

The 80/20 and 20/80 structure reflects a deliberate asymmetry in the portfolio manager's objectives. Maintaining a 20% equity allocation even during bear markets acknowledges several realities. First, the model may be wrong: the filtered probability is uncertain, and a regime signal that turns out to be a false alarm should not have forced the portfolio entirely out of equities, missing the subsequent recovery. Second, bear markets contain the most violent up-days in market history, i.e. the reflex rallies that punctuate drawdowns, and a zero-equity allocation forfeits any participation in these moves. Third, many institutional investors have mandate constraints that require a minimum equity allocation.

Conversely, maintaining only 80% equity during bull markets (rather than 100% or a leveraged position) reflects a conservative stance: the risk-free allocation provides a buffer against model uncertainty and ensures that even if the model fails to detect a regime transition in time, the portfolio has some protection. The 20% risk-free allocation during bull markets earns a positive yield (4% per annum in the current calibration) rather than sitting idle, which cushions the cost of not being fully invested.

### Where the value comes from

The strategy's value does not come from predicting market direction. It comes from the mathematical relationship between the two regimes' volatilities. Bear months have roughly twice the volatility of bull months. Reducing equity exposure by 60 percentage points (from 80% to 20%) during these months reduces the portfolio's contribution from bear-market volatility by a factor of four ($0.2^2 / 0.8^2 = 1/16$ in variance terms for the equity component), while the reduction in expected return is proportionally smaller because the bear-regime mean is negative or near zero. The net effect on the Sharpe ratio is positive: the denominator (volatility) falls by more than the numerator (mean return), because the high-volatility months are also the low-return months.

The effect on maximum drawdown is even more pronounced. Drawdowns accumulate during sustained sequences of bear-regime months. By reducing equity exposure at the onset of a detected bear regime, the strategy limits the accumulation of losses during the worst periods. Even with a detection delay of two to four months, the remaining duration of the bear regime is spent with reduced exposure, resulting in a materially shallower drawdown. The Calmar ratio (annualised return divided by maximum drawdown) improves because the shallower drawdown more than compensates for any reduction in annualised return.

### Important caveats on the current implementation

The current implementation operates within several limitations that a production deployment would need to address. The posterior parameters are estimated on the full sample, so even though the allocation uses filtered probabilities (which are causal), the model parameters carry indirect information about the full sample period. This makes the demonstration informative about the *mechanism* of regime-aware allocation but unreliable as an estimate of out-of-sample performance. A proper evaluation requires walk-forward re-estimation, in which the model is fitted only on data available up to each decision point, with no future leakage into the parameter estimates.

Transaction costs and market impact are ignored. In practice, shifting from 80% to 20% equity in a single month involves selling 75% of the equity position. For large portfolios, this generates significant transaction costs and potential market impact. The frequency of regime-switching signals and the magnitude of the weight changes would need to be calibrated against realistic cost assumptions.

The 80/20 and 20/80 weights are illustrative. In a production setting, these would be optimised against the investor's risk budget, liability structure, and benchmark constraints. The binary threshold at $P(\text{Bear}) = 0.5$ could also be replaced with a continuous mapping from regime probability to equity weight, which would reduce turnover and smooth the allocation path.

## 6. Scenario Simulation and Stress Testing

Regime-switching models are particularly well suited to scenario analysis because they provide a natural language for stress-testing questions. Rather than asking "what happens if volatility doubles?", a question that a single-regime model can answer only by ad hoc parameter perturbation, a regime-switching model allows the analyst to ask "what happens if the market enters a bear regime and stays there for twelve months?" This question maps directly onto the model's structure: it corresponds to pinning $s_t = \text{Bear}$ for a specified horizon and simulating forward from the bear-regime emission distribution.

### Baseline forward simulation

The baseline scenario propagates all sources of uncertainty forward. For each posterior draw $\theta^{(m)}$:

1. Sample the initial regime from the filtered distribution at the end of the estimation period.
2. Simulate a regime path $s_{T+1}, s_{T+2}, \ldots, s_{T+H}$ from the transition matrix.
3. For each simulated regime, draw returns from the corresponding emission distribution: $\mathbf{r}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s_t}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{s_t})$.
4. Compute portfolio metrics along the simulated path.

Repeating this for many posterior draws produces a distribution of outcomes that reflects both parameter uncertainty (variation across $\theta^{(m)}$) and regime uncertainty (variation across simulated regime paths). The result is not a single forecast but a fan chart of possible futures, from which risk metrics such as VaR, CVaR, maximum drawdown distributions, and Sharpe/Calmar ratio distributions can be extracted.

### Stress scenarios

The model's structure enables several natural stress tests:

**Regime pinning** fixes the regime to a specified state for a given horizon. Pinning the bear regime for six or twelve months answers the question: "If we are entering a sustained downturn, what is the distribution of portfolio losses?" This is more informative than a simple volatility shock because it captures both the elevated volatility *and* the negative expected return of the stress regime, as well as the correlation structure if regime-dependent correlations are enabled.

**Mean shifts** perturb the regime-conditional mean vector. Adding $\Delta\boldsymbol{\mu}$ to the bear-regime mean simulates a more severe downturn than the historical average bear market. This addresses the concern that future crises may be worse than those in the estimation sample.

**Covariance scaling** multiplies a regime's covariance matrix by a scalar $\lambda > 1$. This simulates a volatility environment more extreme than the historical bear regime, which is useful for stress-testing against a scenario like the 2008 crisis, which was more severe than the median bear market.

These scenarios can be combined. A "severe and prolonged crisis" scenario might pin the bear regime for twelve months, shift the mean down by an additional 2% monthly, and scale the covariance by 1.5. The resulting loss distribution provides a concrete, model-consistent answer to the question of how bad things could get.

## 7. Planned Extensions and Their Financial Motivation

Each planned extension addresses a specific limitation of the current model that matters for practical portfolio management. The extensions are ordered by increasing complexity, and each builds on the infrastructure of the previous one.

### 7.1 Scenario forward simulation and performance evaluation (Notebook 02)

The first extension implements the scenario analysis framework described in Section 6. Beyond the mechanical implementation, the financial motivation is to transform the model from a descriptive tool (it identifies regimes) into a prescriptive one (it quantifies the distribution of future outcomes under different assumptions). A portfolio manager who knows the current regime probability has useful information; a portfolio manager who also has a distribution of twelve-month-ahead portfolio metrics (VaR, CVaR, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio) conditioned on different regime scenarios has a tool for making allocation decisions and communicating risk to stakeholders.

The performance evaluation component addresses a practical concern: how should regime-aware strategies be evaluated? Standard backtesting metrics (Sharpe, Calmar, maximum drawdown) provide summary statistics, but they do not capture the distribution of these metrics across parameter uncertainty. A Bayesian approach produces a *posterior distribution* of the Sharpe ratio, reflecting uncertainty about whether the observed improvement over a static benchmark is genuine or within the range of sampling variation. This is a more honest assessment than a single point estimate with a bootstrap confidence interval, because it integrates over parameter uncertainty rather than conditioning on a plug-in estimate.

### 7.2 Regime-dependent correlations, Student-t emissions, and AR dynamics (Notebook 03)

**Regime-dependent correlations.** The current model assumes that assets are conditionally independent given the regime ($\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{I}$). This means all observed co-movement comes from the shared regime label: when the model assigns two months to the same regime, it treats the returns of different assets in that month as independent draws from their respective marginals. In reality, assets exhibit substantial within-regime correlation. US and European equities move together even within a bull market, driven by shared macro exposure, sector overlap, and capital flows. More importantly, within-regime correlations differ *between* regimes. The correlation between US and EM equities might be 0.5 during bull markets and 0.85 during bear markets, reflecting the well-documented phenomenon of correlation convergence during stress.

Ignoring regime-dependent correlations has two consequences. First, it understates within-regime portfolio risk, because the model assumes diversification benefits that exceed what the data actually provide. Second, it prevents the model from capturing correlation breakdown during crises, which is one of the primary risk-management motivations for regime-switching models in the first place. The extension replaces $\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{I}$ with a full regime-dependent correlation matrix estimated via the LKJ Cholesky parameterisation, with separate correlation matrices for each regime. This allows the model to learn that diversification works better in bull markets than in bear markets, a finding that is central to multi-asset risk management.

**Student-t emissions.** The current model assumes that returns within each regime follow a multivariate Normal distribution. This captures the regime-level differences in mean and volatility but imposes thin tails within each regime. In practice, even conditioning on a single regime, financial returns exhibit excess kurtosis: there are more extreme observations than a Normal distribution predicts. The October 1987 crash, for instance, occurred during a period that most regime-switching models would classify as part of a broader bull market. A Normal emission model treats such an outlier as a near-impossible event; a Student-t emission model with moderate degrees of freedom ($\nu \approx 5$--$8$) assigns it meaningful probability.

The financial implication is direct: a Normal-emission model understates intra-regime tail risk. VaR and CVaR computed from Normal regime-conditional distributions will underestimate the frequency and severity of extreme losses within a given regime. Student-t emissions address this by adding a degrees-of-freedom parameter $\nu_k$ to each regime, allowing the data to determine how heavy the intra-regime tails are. This is particularly important for the bear regime, where tail risk is already elevated and further underestimation is especially costly. The regime-conditional VaR under Student-t emissions is:

$$
\text{VaR}_\alpha^{(k)} = \mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}_k + \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k \, \mathbf{w}} \cdot t_{\nu_k,\, 1-\alpha}
$$

where $t_{\nu_k, 1-\alpha}$ is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the Student-t distribution with $\nu_k$ degrees of freedom. For $\nu_k = 5$ and $\alpha = 0.01$, this quantile is approximately 3.36, compared to 2.33 for the Normal, representing a 44% increase in the VaR estimate that reflects the genuine tail risk present in the data.

**AR dynamics within regimes.** The current model assumes that $\mathbf{y}_t \perp \mathbf{y}_{t-1} \mid s_t$, i.e. given the regime, today's return is independent of yesterday's. This rules out within-regime momentum and mean-reversion effects. In reality, equity returns exhibit weak but exploitable serial dependence: short-term momentum (returns tend to continue for one to twelve months) and long-term reversal (extreme returns over three to five years tend to reverse). These effects may differ between regimes; momentum may be stronger during bull markets, while mean reversion may dominate during bear markets as oversold conditions attract value buyers. Adding AR($p$) dynamics within each regime allows the model to capture these effects and produce more accurate return forecasts at horizons beyond one month.

### 7.3 Time-varying transition probabilities with exogenous covariates (Notebook 04)

The current model assumes that the transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is constant over time: the probability of switching from bull to bear is the same whether the VIX is at 12 or at 35, and whether the yield curve is steeply positive or deeply inverted. This is a strong assumption that ignores a substantial body of evidence linking observable economic variables to the probability of regime transitions.

The VIX (implied volatility of S&P 500 options) is perhaps the most direct market-based indicator of regime. A VIX level below 15 is characteristic of calm bull markets; a spike above 30 signals acute stress. The yield curve slope (difference between long-term and short-term Treasury yields) has a well-documented track record as a recession predictor: inversions have preceded every US recession since the 1960s. Credit spreads, employment growth, and manufacturing survey data all carry predictive information about the probability of a regime transition.

Time-varying transition probabilities (TVTP) incorporate this information by conditioning the transition probabilities on observable covariates:

$$
P(s_t = k \mid s_{t-1} = j, \mathbf{x}_t) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}_{jk})}{\sum_{k'} \exp(\mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}_{jk'})}
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_t$ is a vector of observable variables and $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{jk}$ is a regime-pair-specific coefficient vector. The financial payoff is twofold. First, the model can detect regime transitions earlier, because the covariates may signal a transition before it is apparent in return data alone. A VIX spike or a yield curve inversion may precede the actual drawdown by several months, and a TVTP model can begin increasing the bear probability based on these leading indicators. Second, TVTP enables a richer class of scenario analysis: the analyst can ask "what happens to regime probabilities if the VIX doubles?" or "what if the yield curve inverts?" and receive a model-consistent answer that feeds through to allocation and risk metrics.

### 7.4 Real data and walk-forward evaluation (Notebook 05)

All results to this point use synthetic data generated from a known regime-switching process. This is the correct approach for model validation (it verifies that the inference machinery works as intended) but it does not address the question that matters most to practitioners: does the model add value on real market data?

The final extension applies the full pipeline to historical market data (e.g. S&P 500, MSCI EAFE, MSCI EM) from 2000 to 2025, spanning the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis, the 2020 COVID crash, and the subsequent recoveries. The key methodological requirement is walk-forward re-estimation: at each decision point, the model is fitted only on data available up to that date, and the allocation decision is made using filtered probabilities from the most recent estimation. This eliminates all forms of look-ahead bias (in the parameters, in the regime probabilities, and in the allocation weights) and provides an honest assessment of the strategy's out-of-sample performance.

The walk-forward evaluation also addresses the question of re-estimation frequency. How often should the model be re-fitted? Monthly re-estimation is computationally expensive; annual re-estimation risks stale parameters. The evaluation will explore the trade-off between estimation frequency and performance, with the expectation that re-estimation every six to twelve months, or triggered by a detected regime shift, provides a reasonable balance.

## 8. Limitations and Honest Caveats

A regime-switching model is a significant improvement over a single-regime model for equity risk management, but it is not a complete solution. The following limitations should be understood by any practitioner deploying this framework.

### The number of regimes is assumed, not learned

The current model fixes $K = 2$ by assumption. This is the most common choice in the literature and is supported by the basic intuition of bull and bear markets, but it is not necessarily optimal. Some authors have argued for three regimes (adding a "crash" state with very short duration and extreme negative returns) or even four (separating "normal growth" from "euphoria" in the bull phase). Bayesian model comparison via WAIC or LOO-CV can provide evidence for or against additional regimes, but the current implementation does not perform this comparison. A practitioner should be aware that the two-regime assumption may miss important structure in the data, or, conversely, that fitting more regimes may lead to overfitting and unstable parameter estimates.

### In-sample vs out-of-sample performance

The regime-aware allocation results presented in Notebook 01 use parameters estimated from the full sample, even though the allocation weights are determined by causal (filtered) probabilities. This means the model's parameters, i.e. the regime means, volatilities, and transition probabilities, carry information about the entire sample period, including future data. The filtered probabilities are causal in form but not in substance, because they are conditioned on parameters that were informed by future observations. The Sharpe and Calmar ratio improvements shown in the notebook are therefore upper bounds on what would be achieved in a true out-of-sample setting. Walk-forward re-estimation (Notebook 05) is the only way to produce reliable performance estimates.

### Model mis-specification

The model assumes that returns within each regime follow a specific distribution (currently multivariate Normal, later multivariate Student-t). If the true data-generating process does not conform to this assumption (for example, if within-regime returns exhibit skewness, time-varying volatility, or non-linear dependence) the model's risk estimates will be biased. Posterior predictive checks, in which simulated data from the fitted model are compared against the observed data on key summary statistics, can detect some forms of mis-specification. But they cannot detect all forms, and a model that passes posterior predictive checks may still perform poorly out of sample if the nature of the mis-specification changes.

### Regime detection delay

The forward filter requires several observations from a new regime before it can assign high probability to the regime change. In practice, this means that the first two to four months of a bear market may not be identified as such until the drawdown is already well under way. For the 2008 financial crisis, for example, the S&P 500 peaked in October 2007 but the sharpest declines occurred in September--November 2008. A filter that detects the regime change by early 2008 would provide useful warning; one that does not detect it until late 2008 would provide no value. The detection speed depends on the magnitude of the regime difference (larger differences are detected faster), the transition probability (high persistence makes the filter slower to accept that a transition has occurred), and the noise level (higher volatility makes individual observations less informative).

### Stationarity assumptions

The model assumes that the regime parameters ($\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$) and the transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ are constant over the entire sample period. In reality, the character of bull and bear markets may change over time. The bear markets of the 1970s (driven by inflation and oil shocks) had different statistical characteristics from the bear markets of the 2000s (driven by financial leverage and contagion). If the parameters drift over time, the model estimated on a long historical sample may not accurately characterise the current regime. Rolling or expanding estimation windows can mitigate this concern, but they introduce their own trade-off between sample size (longer windows give more precise estimates) and relevance (shorter windows are more responsive to parameter drift).

### Transaction costs and implementation

The allocation strategy involves shifting substantial capital between equities and risk-free assets in response to regime signals. In the current implementation, the weight can change from 80% equity to 20% equity in a single month, a movement of 60 percentage points, corresponding to selling 75% of the equity position. For institutional-scale portfolios, this generates significant transaction costs (brokerage, bid-ask spreads, market impact) that are not modelled. The strategy's net-of-cost performance may differ materially from the gross figures reported. Moreover, the binary threshold at $P(\text{Bear}) = 0.5$ can produce rapid switching when the filtered probability oscillates near the boundary, generating high turnover. A practical implementation would need to address this through a smoother mapping from probability to weight, a hysteresis band around the threshold, or minimum holding periods.

### Label switching and identifiability

The regime labels (0 and 1, or "bull" and "bear") are arbitrary: permuting the labels and the corresponding parameters produces an identical likelihood. This label-switching symmetry creates a multimodal posterior with $K!$ equivalent modes. Different MCMC chains may explore different modes, producing seemingly divergent parameter estimates that are actually the same model with permuted labels. The current implementation addresses this through post-hoc permutation alignment, which works well for $K = 2$ but becomes more complex for larger $K$. This is a technical rather than substantive limitation (the regime probabilities and portfolio allocations are invariant to the labelling) but it can complicate diagnostics and model interpretation if not handled carefully.
